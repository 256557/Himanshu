# Compensatory evolution with a costly compensator

## Question
A bacterial lineage fixes a mutation that confers antibiotic resistance but reduces growth rate in the absence of the drug. During later propagation without the antibiotic, growth rate recovers almost fully, yet the resistance mutation is retained. The second-site mutation responsible for the recovery reduces growth rate when introduced alone into the ancestral, drug-sensitive background. Explain how such a mutation can spread, what form of epistasis this implies, and why the lineage does not subsequently lose the resistance mutation.

## Answer outline
1. The compensator is beneficial only on the resistant background. When the resistance mutation is already fixed, the compensator arises on that background and is favoured, so its cost in the ancestral background never enters the dynamics.
2. Its effect changes sign with genetic background, which is sign epistasis. The double mutant has higher fitness than the resistant single mutant, and the compensator alone has lower fitness than the ancestor.
3. Reversion of the resistance mutation would now produce the compensator-only genotype, which is less fit than the double mutant. Reversion is therefore selected against, and the lineage sits on a separate fitness peak. Resistance persists even without the drug.
4. Compensation is reached more often than reversion because many sites can compensate, whereas reversion requires one specific change, as discussed in the previous question.

## Likely wrong answers
That a mutation which is harmful alone cannot spread; that the resistance mutation will be lost once the drug is removed; or that the two mutations act additively.
