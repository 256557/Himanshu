# Why a protein can retain its native structure but lose catalytic activity

## Principle

The native fold and catalytic activity depend on different parts of the protein. The fold is stabilised by many interactions distributed over the whole chain: packing of the hydrophobic core, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges and, in some proteins, disulfide bonds. Catalysis depends on the chemical identity, protonation state and precise geometry of a small number of active-site groups, often between two and five residues and any bound cofactor. A change confined to those groups can lower activity to undetectable levels while the fold, judged by circular dichroism or a crystal structure, remains native.

## Mechanisms

### Substitution of a catalytic residue

In chymotrypsin the catalytic triad is Ser195, His57 and Asp102. Replacing Ser195 with alanine removes the nucleophilic hydroxyl that forms the acyl-enzyme intermediate. The substitution is small and does not disturb the core, so the protein folds normally. Activity falls by several orders of magnitude; mutant serine proteases studied in this way retain a small residual activity above the uncatalysed rate, so the loss is near-complete rather than absolute.

### Covalent modification of a catalytic residue

Diisopropyl fluorophosphate reacts with Ser195 to form a diisopropylphosphoryl ester:

Ser195-OH + (iPrO)2P(O)F -> Ser195-O-P(O)(O-iPr)2 + HF

The modified serine can no longer act as a nucleophile and the phosphoryl group occupies the active site. The modification is covalent and irreversible, and the fold is unchanged.

### Loss of a catalytic metal or cofactor

In many metalloenzymes the metal acts as a Lewis acid, activates a water molecule or stabilises charge in the transition state. In carbonic anhydrase, for example, a zinc ion bound by three histidines lowers the pKa of a bound water so that it can act as a nucleophile towards carbon dioxide. Removing the metal, for example by chelation, gives an inactive apoenzyme. The fold is retained when the metal has a catalytic role only. When it also holds parts of the chain together, as structural zinc sites do in many proteins, its removal can destabilise the fold, so this route separates structure from activity only in the first case. Restoring the metal to a correctly folded apoenzyme restores activity, which is itself evidence that the fold survived.

### Protonation state of catalytic groups

Catalytic groups act only in a particular protonation state. The triad histidine (pKa near 7 in chymotrypsin) must be neutral to act as a general base and is largely protonated below pH 5. Papain-like cysteine proteases require a thiolate-imidazolium pair. At pH values outside the activity range but within the stability range of the fold, activity is lost while the structure is retained, and activity returns when the pH is restored.

### Occupancy of the active site

A tight-binding competitive inhibitor blocks the substrate site without altering the fold; the enzyme regains activity when the inhibitor dissociates.

### Loss of required motion

A substitution in a hinge can block a conformational change that the catalytic cycle requires, such as closure of a lid domain over the substrate, leaving an inactive protein whose static structure matches the native one.

## Conclusion

Structure and activity separate because the fold is a property of the whole chain, whereas catalysis is a property of a few precisely arranged groups and, in some enzymes, their motions. Any change confined to those groups, including substitution, covalent modification, loss of a catalytic metal, an unsuitable protonation state, occupancy by an inhibitor or loss of required motion, can reduce activity to near zero while the native fold is maintained.
