# Set 9 Q3: model answer

## Principle
Any cell present during the exposure could carry a direct effect of the compound, so the cut-off is the first generation with no such cell.

## Step 1. Exposure of a pregnant female
When a pregnant female is exposed, three generations are in contact with the endocrine disruptor at once: the mother (F0), the embryo she carries (F1), and the primordial germ cells inside that embryo, which will later give rise to the F2.

A phenotype in the F1 or F2 can therefore reflect direct exposure. Only a phenotype that persists into the F3 can be attributed to transgenerational inheritance.

## Step 2. Exposure of an adult male
Exposure of a male before mating reaches him and his sperm, so the F1, which develops from an exposed sperm, is still directly affected.

The germ cells of that F1 arise after the exposure has ended, which makes the F2 the first unexposed generation.

## Step 3. What adaptation would require
Adaptation needs heritable differences in fitness that last long enough for selection to change the composition of a population.

A change induced in every exposed individual is a plastic response, and it becomes adaptive only if it is transmitted stably and favoured over the alternative state.

## Step 4. The mammalian germline barrier
DNA methylation is erased in primordial germ cells and again after fertilisation, and most sperm histones are replaced by protamines.

A mark must therefore either escape both rounds of erasure or be rebuilt in each generation, for example by small RNAs carried in sperm.

## Step 5. The mammalian evidence
Some retrotransposon-derived and imprinted regions escape erasure in part, and sperm RNAs can alter the phenotype of offspring. Injection of such RNAs into zygotes, however, tests only the next generation and so shows an intergenerational effect. Most reported effects nevertheless fade by the F2 or F3, many endocrine-disruptor phenotypes are diseases that lower fitness, and human data such as the Överkalix records are correlational.

## Step 6. Evidence outside mammals
In C. elegans, silencing that depends on small RNAs can persist for several generations, and in plants some epialleles stay stable for many generations and segregate much like alleles. In these organisms selection could act on an epigenetic variant without any change in DNA sequence.

## Conclusion
The phenotype counts as transgenerational from the F3 after gestational exposure and from the F2 after paternal exposure. In mammals, adaptation through epigenetic inheritance remains possible in principle but has not been demonstrated, because the reported effects are short-lived and often harmful.
